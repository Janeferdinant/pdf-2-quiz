Social stratification is a society's categorization of its people into socioeconomic tiers based on factors such as wealth, income, education, occupation, and social status. Sociologists emphasize that stratification is a trait of society, not simply a reflection of individual differences; it persists over generations and is universally present across human cultures, although its specific structures vary.

Systems of stratification are broadly categorized as either closed or open. Closed systems accommodate little to no change in social position; they prescribe an individual's status at birth (ascribed status). The classic illustration is a caste system, where social standing is permanent, endogamous marriage within one's caste is strictly enforced, and occupational pathways are tightly circumscribed. Open systems, in contrast, are based on achievement and allow for social mobility. A class system is an open system based on both social factors and individual achievement, where status can be altered through education, economic success, or career advancement.

Social mobility refers to the ability of individuals or groups to shift their position within a social hierarchy. Intergenerational mobility describes a difference in social class between different generations of a family, such as a child of factory workers becoming a physician. Intragenerational mobility refers to changes in an individual's social standing within their own adult lifetime.

Sociological perspectives offer divergent explanations for social inequality. The functionalist perspective, notably articulated in the Davis-Moore thesis (1945), posits that social stratification has beneficial consequences for society. Functionalists argue that rewarding difficult, highly skilled, or socially critical occupations with greater prestige and income ensures that the most qualified individuals pursue demanding roles. In sharp contrast, conflict theorists, influenced by Karl Marx, argue that stratification perpetuates economic exploitation and inequality. Marx analyzed society as divided between the bourgeoisie (who own the means of production) and the proletariat (workers who sell their labor), with stratification serving the interests of dominant elites at the expense of working classes.
