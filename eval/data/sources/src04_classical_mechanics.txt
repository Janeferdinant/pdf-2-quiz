Classical Newtonian mechanics describes the motion of macroscopic objects moving at speeds substantially lower than the speed of light. Sir Isaac Newton formulated three foundational laws of motion in the Philosophiæ Naturalis Principia Mathematica.

Newton's First Law of Motion, the law of inertia, states that an object at rest remains at rest, and an object in uniform motion continues moving at constant velocity in a straight line, unless acted upon by an unbalanced external net force. Inertia is an intrinsic property of matter directly proportional to an object's mass.

Newton's Second Law of Motion establishes the quantitative relationship between force, mass, and acceleration. It states that the net force acting on an object is equal to the rate of change of its linear momentum with respect to time: F_net = dp/dt. For systems with constant mass, this reduces to the familiar vector equation F_net = ma, where force and acceleration share the same direction. The SI unit of force is the newton (N), defined as one kilogram-meter per second squared.

Newton's Third Law of Motion asserts that when object A exerts a force on object B, object B simultaneously exerts an equal and opposite force on object A: F_AB = -F_BA. Action and reaction forces always act on different bodies and therefore never cancel each other out.

Linear momentum (p) is the product of an object's mass and its velocity: p = mv. Momentum is a vector quantity having the same direction as the velocity. In an isolated system experiencing no external net force, the total momentum remains conserved regardless of internal interactions. In elastic collisions, both total momentum and total kinetic energy are conserved. In inelastic collisions, total momentum is conserved, but kinetic energy is partially converted into thermal energy, sound, or structural deformation. In a completely inelastic collision, the interacting bodies stick together after impact and move with a common final velocity.
