Gross Domestic Product (GDP) is the primary metric used by macroeconomists to measure the monetary value of all finished goods and services produced within a nation's geographic boundaries during a specified time interval, typically a quarter or a year. GDP counts only final goods and services to avoid double counting intermediate goods used as inputs in production.

Economists measure GDP using either the expenditure approach or the national income approach, which yield identical totals due to the circular flow of income. Under the expenditure approach, GDP is decomposed into four primary components: Consumption (C), Business Investment (I), Government Purchases (G), and Net Exports (NX = Exports - Imports). Thus, GDP = C + I + G + (X - M). Consumption by households typically accounts for the largest fraction of GDP in developed market economies, often exceeding 65% in the United States.

Nominal GDP evaluates national production using the actual prices prevailing in the year the output was produced. Consequently, nominal GDP can rise purely because of inflation rather than an increase in real output. To distinguish between physical growth and price inflation, economists calculate Real GDP, which measures total production using constant base-year prices. The GDP deflator is a price index reflecting the overall price level of all domestically produced goods and services, calculated as (Nominal GDP / Real GDP) multiplied by 100.

While Real GDP per capita is frequently utilized as a proxy for economic living standards, it exhibits well-documented limitations as an indicator of human well-being. GDP does not capture non-market economic activities, such as unpaid care work, household labor, or informal economic transactions. Additionally, GDP fails to account for environmental degradation, resource depletion, leisure time, income inequality, or the distribution of output among citizens.
