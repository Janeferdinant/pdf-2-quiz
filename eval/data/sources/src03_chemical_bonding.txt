Atoms combine to form stable compounds through chemical bonds, driven by the thermodynamic imperative to minimize electrostatic potential energy and attain noble gas valence electron configurations. The three primary classifications of chemical bonds are ionic, covalent, and metallic bonds.

Ionic bonding occurs primarily between elements with large differences in electronegativity, typically metals and nonmetals. Metals, having low ionization energies and low electronegativity, readily lose valence electrons to form positively charged cations. Nonmetals, possessing high electron affinities and high electronegativity, accept electrons to form negatively charged anions. The electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions binds them into three-dimensional crystalline lattice networks. The stability of an ionic lattice is quantified by its lattice energy, which represents the energy released when gaseous ions coalesce into an ionic solid.

Covalent bonding occurs when atoms with similar electronegativities share one or more pairs of valence electrons. In nonpolar covalent bonds, such as in diatomic nitrogen (N2) or methane (CH4), electrons are shared equally between atoms because the electronegativity difference is negligible (typically less than 0.4 on the Pauling scale). In polar covalent bonds, such as in water (H2O) or hydrogen chloride (HCl), the electronegativity difference (typically between 0.4 and 1.8) results in unequal sharing, creating a dipole moment where partial negative charge resides on the more electronegative atom and partial positive charge resides on the less electronegative atom.

Valence shell electron pair repulsion (VSEPR) theory predicts the three-dimensional geometry of molecules based on the repulsion between electron pairs surrounding a central atom. Lone pairs exert greater repulsive forces than bonding pairs, distorting ideal bond angles. For example, water possesses four electron groups around its oxygen atom (two bonding pairs and two lone pairs), yielding a tetrahedral electron geometry but a bent molecular geometry with a bond angle of 104.5 degrees, compressed from the ideal tetrahedral angle of 109.5 degrees.
