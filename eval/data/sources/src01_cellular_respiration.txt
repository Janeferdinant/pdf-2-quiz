Cellular respiration is a collection of three metabolic pathways: glycolysis, the citric acid cycle, and oxidative phosphorylation. Glycolysis occurs in the cytoplasm of both prokaryotic and eukaryotic cells and does not require oxygen, meaning it is an anaerobic process. During glycolysis, a single six-carbon glucose molecule is broken down into two three-carbon molecules of pyruvate. This process consumes two molecules of ATP during the energy investment phase and produces four molecules of ATP and two molecules of NADH during the energy payoff phase, resulting in a net yield of two ATP and two NADH per glucose molecule.

In the presence of oxygen, pyruvate enters the mitochondrial matrix where it undergoes pyruvate oxidation. The pyruvate dehydrogenase complex removes a carboxyl group from pyruvate, releasing carbon dioxide, and attaches the remaining two-carbon acetyl group to coenzyme A to form acetyl-CoA, producing one molecule of NADH per pyruvate.

Acetyl-CoA then enters the citric acid cycle (also known as the Krebs cycle or tricarboxylic acid cycle) within the mitochondrial matrix. In the first step, oxaloacetate (a four-carbon molecule) combines with acetyl-CoA to produce citrate (a six-carbon molecule). Over a series of enzymatic reactions, two carbon atoms are released as carbon dioxide, three molecules of NAD+ are reduced to NADH, one molecule of FAD is reduced to FADH2, and one molecule of GTP (or ATP) is generated via substrate-level phosphorylation per acetyl group. Because each glucose molecule produces two pyruvates, the cycle turns twice per glucose.

The final stage of aerobic respiration is oxidative phosphorylation, which takes place on the inner mitochondrial membrane (cristae). This stage consists of the electron transport chain (ETC) and chemiosmosis. The electron transport chain consists of four protein complexes (Complexes I through IV) embedded in the cristae membrane. High-energy electrons donated by NADH and FADH2 pass sequentially through these complexes. As electrons move through Complexes I, III, and IV, protons (H+) are pumped from the mitochondrial matrix into the intermembrane space, creating an electrochemical proton gradient known as the proton motive force. Molecular oxygen (O2) acts as the terminal electron acceptor at Complex IV, combining with electrons and protons to form metabolic water (H2O).

In chemiosmosis, protons flow down their electrochemical gradient back into the mitochondrial matrix through ATP synthase. The flow of protons rotates the rotor subunit of ATP synthase, driving the phosphorylation of ADP to generate ATP. Under optimal cellular conditions, oxidative phosphorylation yields approximately 26 to 28 ATP molecules per glucose molecule, bringing the theoretical total maximum yield of aerobic respiration to approximately 30 to 32 ATP per glucose molecule.
