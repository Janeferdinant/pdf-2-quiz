Stars are self-luminous celestial spheres of plasma held in hydrostatic equilibrium, where the outward thermal pressure generated by nuclear fusion balances the inward gravitational collapse. The Hertzsprung-Russell (H-R) diagram plots stellar luminosity against surface temperature, classifying stars into distinct developmental stages.

A star begins its life cycle inside a dense interstellar molecular cloud of cold gas and dust. Gravitational contraction of a core fragment generates heat until core temperatures reach approximately 10 million Kelvin, triggering the fusion of hydrogen into helium via the proton-proton chain or the CNO cycle. At this point, the star enters the Main Sequence, where it spends approximately 90% of its lifetime. A star's initial mass is the single most critical parameter dictating its luminosity, core temperature, fuel consumption rate, and ultimate demise.

For intermediate-mass stars like our Sun (masses below approximately 8 solar masses), core hydrogen depletion leads to core contraction and outer envelope expansion, transforming the star into a Red Giant. Once core temperatures rise to roughly 100 million Kelvin, the triple-alpha process begins, fusing helium into carbon and oxygen. After helium is exhausted, the star cannot achieve the high temperatures required to fuse carbon. The outer atmospheric layers are gently expelled as an expanding fluorescent planetary nebula, leaving behind the inert, dense degenerate core: a White Dwarf. White dwarfs are supported against gravitational collapse by electron degeneracy pressure and gradually cool over billions of years.

High-mass stars (greater than 8 solar masses) proceed through successive stages of advanced nuclear burning, creating concentric shells of neon, oxygen, and silicon, terminating with an iron (Fe) core. Iron fusion is endothermic and absorbs energy rather than releasing it, abruptly extinguishing outward radiation pressure. The core catastrophically collapses in milliseconds, triggering a Type II core-collapse Supernova explosion that disperses heavy elements across the interstellar medium. The remnant core collapses into either a ultra-dense Neutron Star (supported by neutron degeneracy pressure) or, for progenitor stars with cores exceeding the Tolman-Oppenheimer-Volkoff limit (roughly 2.2 to 3 solar masses), an inescapable Black Hole.
