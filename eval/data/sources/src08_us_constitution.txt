The Constitution of the United States, drafted at the Constitutional Convention in Philadelphia in 1787 and ratified in 1788, serves as the supreme law of the nation. It established a federal republic characterized by constitutionalism, the separation of powers, checks and balances, and federalism.

To prevent tyranny and arbitrary accumulation of authority, the Framers divided governmental powers horizontally among three independent branches across the first three Articles. Article I vests legislative authority in a bicameral Congress comprising the Senate and the House of Representatives. Article II vests executive power in the President of the United States, charged with executing laws and serving as Commander in Chief. Article III establishes the federal judiciary, headed by the Supreme Court.

The institutional framework includes an intricate system of checks and balances enabling each branch to restrain the actions of the others. The President may veto legislation passed by Congress; Congress may override a presidential veto with a two-thirds supermajority in both chambers. The Senate possesses the power of advice and consent, confirming presidential appointments of ambassadors, federal judges, and cabinet officers, as well as ratifying international treaties by a two-thirds vote. Although not explicitly stated in the constitutional text, the principle of judicial review—the authority of courts to invalidate legislative and executive acts contrary to the Constitution—was firmly established by Chief Justice John Marshall in the landmark Supreme Court decision Marbury v. Madison (1803).

Federalism represents the vertical distribution of power between the national government and individual state governments. Delegated or enumerated powers, such as regulating interstate commerce and coining money, belong to the national government under Article I, Section 8. Under the Tenth Amendment, powers not delegated to the United States by the Constitution, nor prohibited by it to the states, are reserved to the states respectively, or to the people.
